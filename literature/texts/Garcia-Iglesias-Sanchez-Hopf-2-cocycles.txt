Abstract. We present a framework for the computation of the Hopf 2-cocycles
involved in the deformations of Nichols algebras over semisimple Hopf algebras.
We write down a recurrence formula and investigate the extent of the connection with invariant Hochschild cohomology in terms of exponentials.
As an example, we present detailed computations leading to the explicit
description of the Hopf 2-cocycles involved in the deformations of a Nichols
algebra of Cartan type A2 with q = −1, a.k.a. the positive part of the small
√ (sl3 ). We show that these cocycles are generically pure,

1. Introduction
Let A = (A, m, ∆) be a Hopf algebra over a field k. A Hopf cocycle σ with
coefficients in k for A is a linear map σ : A ⊗ A → k which is convolution invertible
and satisfies
σ(x(1) , y(1) )σ(x(2) y(2) , z) = σ(y(1) , z(1) )σ(x, y(2) z(2) ),

x, y, z ∈ A.

This condition allows to deform the multiplication m
mσ = σ ∗ m ∗ σ −1 into
a new associative product, so that the cocycle deformation Aσ = (A, mσ , ∆) is
again a Hopf algebra. Introduced by Doi and Takeuchi in [DT], Hopf cocycles have
evolved in recent years from a technical device to an inescapable and key tool in
the classification program for non-semisimple Hopf algebras.
Indeed, the classification of finite-dimensional pointed Hopf algebras over abelian groups is completely determined by Hopf cocycles. Each such Hopf algebra
is a cocycle deformation of the bosonization B#H, where B = B(V ) is a finitedimensional Nichols algebra of diagonal type and H = kΓ is a commutative group
algebra. This was first observed by Masuoka in [Ma], see also [GrM], for the Hopf
algebras classified in [AS2], and proved in full generality in [AnG]. A similar statement is true for every known example of a finite-dimensional pointed Hopf algebra
over H = kG, G non-necessarily abelian, cf. [GaM, GIM, GV1, GV2]. Moreover,
the same holds for copointed Hopf algebras over H = kG , see [AV, FGM, GV2].
It is important to remark that, despite the name, Hopf cocycles do not arise
from a cohomology setup (unless the Hopf algebra is cocommutative, in which case
there are no deformations in this sense). In fact, the set Z 2 (A) of Hopf cocycles
in A is not a group. This is a major obstruction for a systematic construction of
cocycles. There is, however, a notion of cohomologous cocycles, via an action of
the group of convolution units in A∗ .
As a matter of fact, the classification results bypass an explicit description of the
cocycles involved and work with the algebraic counterpart of cleft objects. These
are certain comodule algebras C endowed with a convolution invertible comodule
isomorphism γ : A → C. Any pair (C, γ) defines a Hopf cocycle via
(1.1)

σ(a, b) = γ(a(1) )γ(b(1) )γ −1 (a(2) b(2) ),
1

a, b ∈ A.

There is a more cohomological alternative to compute Hopf cocycles, rooted in
the Hochschild cohomology H• (A, k) of A, as some Hopf cocycles can be obtained as
exponentials of Hochschild 2-cocycles in Z2 (A, k). We also investigate this approach,
which turns out to be rather limited. We say that a Hopf cocycle is pure when it
is not cohomologous to an exponential.
We propose to explicitly compute the Hopf cocycles involved in the classification
by writing down the section for each cleft object and calculating the values of σ
using (1.1). Also, we determine which of these cocycles are pure and which are
exponentials.

1.3. About the exponential. The theorem above shows in particular that the
exponential approach is far from optimal. Up to cohomology, only three non-trivial
cocycles are exponentials, namely σ(1,0,0) , σ(0,1,0) and σ(0,0,1) .
On the other hand, it is unclear which conditions on η are necessary to imply that
the exponential eη is a Hopf cocycle. There are well-known sufficient commutation
conditions (5.1) and (5.2) in the convolution algebra Hom(A⊗3 , k). As a byproduct
of our calculations, we obtain in Lemma 6.11 that
• There are Hochschild cocycles η that fail (5.1), (5.2) but still eη is a Hopf cocycle.
• However, we show that in our examples such cocycles η are cohomologous to a
cocycle satisfying those conditions. We are not aware if this a general fact.
An important comment worth making is that, depending on the realization and
certain normalization conditions, a pure cocycle deformation Aσ can be isomorphic
to an exponential deformation Aeη , see Remark 6.2. Nevertheless, the corresponding cleft objects are not isomorphic.
Some of these observations have already been implicitly pointed in [GaM], for
cocycles related to pointed Hopf algebras over S4 . Nevertheless, the exponential
Hopf cocycles obtained in loc.cit. considered a restricted class of Hochschild cocycles, namely those that are concentrated in degree one. We hope to obtain a full
description of the missing pure cocycles in future work.
On the other hand, those restrictions are enough to find all cocycles for pointed
Hopf algebras over S3 ibid., as deformations of the Fomin-Kirillov algebra F K3 and
copointed Hopf algebras over the dihedral groups D4m in [FGM]. In a forthcoming
work [GS], we show that pure cocycles are indeed needed for pointed deformations
of F K3 over more general groups G ։ S3 , or for copointed deformations over kS3 .

2. Preliminaries
We work over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero k. All tensor
products, vector spaces and maps are assumed to be defined over k. If θ ∈ N, we
set Iθ = {1, . . . , θ} ⊂ N.
We let (H, m, ∆) be a Hopf algebra; we use Sweedler’s notation ∆(h) = h(1) ⊗h(2)
for the comultiplication, similarly for comodules over H and the coaction. We set
∆(2) = (∆ ⊗ id)∆(= (id ⊗∆)∆). We write G(H) for the set of group-like elements
on H, P(H) for the primitive elements and U (H) for the units. We denote by H0
the coradical of H. Recall that H is pointed when H0 = kG(H) and copointed
when H0 = k G for some (finite) non-abelian group G.
A (right) cleft object for H is a right comodule algebra C endowed with a
convolution invertible comodule isomorphism γ : H → C. This map is called a
section when γ(1) = 1. We let Cleft(H) denote the set of cleft objects up to
isomorphism. Left, and bi-cleft, objects are defined similarly.
2.1. Hopf cocycles. A Hopf 2-cocycle σ : H ⊗ H → k is a convolution-invertible
linear map x ⊗ y 7→ σ(x, y) satisfying
(2.1)

σ(x(1) , y(1) )σ(x(2) y(2) , z) = σ(y(1) , z(1) )σ(x, y(2) z(2) ),

x, y, z ∈ H.

It follows that σ(1, 1) 6= 0 and σ(x, 1) = σ(1, x) = ǫ(x)σ(1, 1), x ∈ H, so by
(2.2)

σ ↔ σ(1, 1)−1 σ

we can always assume that σ(x, 1) = σ(1, x) = ǫ(x). Such cocycles are called
normalized. We shall denote by
Z 2 (H) = {σ : H ⊗ H → k | (2.1) holds}
the set of normalized Hopf 2-cocycles in H.
We will use a coordinate-free version of (2.1), namely
(2.3)

(σ ⊗ ǫ) ∗ σ(m ⊗ id) = (ǫ ⊗ σ) ∗ σ(id ⊗ m).

This allows us to consider a braided version of Hopf cocycles for any bialgebra in a
braided category; see [AG] for details.

2.1.1. Cocycle deformations. Let σ ∈ Z 2 (H). Then ·σ : H ⊗ H → H, given by
(2.4)

x ·σ y = σ(x(1) , y(1) )x(2) y(2) σ −1 (x(3) , y(3) ),

x, y ∈ H,

defines an associative product on the vector space H. Moreover, the collection
(H, ·σ , ∆) is a Hopf algebra with antipode Sσ = f ∗ S ∗ f −1 , for f = σ ◦ (id ⊗ S) ◦ ∆.
This Hopf algebra is denoted Hσ and referred to as a cocycle deformation of H.
If σ ∈ Z 2 (H), then there is another deformation of the multiplication in H.
Namely, ·(σ) : H ⊗ H → H, given by
(2.5)

x ·(σ) y = σ(x(1) , y(1) )x(2) y(2) ,

x, y, ∈ H,

also defines an associative product on the vector space H; we denote this new
algebra by H(σ) . In this case, the comultiplication H → H ⊗ H defines coactions
H(σ) → H(σ) ⊗ H and H(σ) → Hσ ⊗ H(σ) in such a way that C = H(σ) becomes a
(Hσ , H)-bicleft object.
Moreover, any (right) cleft object C for H (and hence any cocycle deformation
Hσ ) arises in this way. If C is a cleft object with a normalized section γ : H → C,
then C ≃ H(σ) ; for the cocycle σ : H ⊗ H → k determined by the formula
(2.6)

σ(x, y) = γ(x(1) )γ(y(1) )γ −1 (x(2) y(2) ),

x, y ∈ H.

We stress that formula (2.6) extends to cleft objects in braided categories, see [AG].
Furthermore, it is possible to recover Hσ completely in terms of the cleft object
C, as the so-called Schauenburg left Hopf algebra L(C, H); in particular, no cocycles
are explicitly involved. See [Sc] for further details.
